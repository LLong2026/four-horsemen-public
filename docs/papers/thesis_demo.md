# Predictive Maintenance Scheduling for Small Manufacturers

## Abstract

This study evaluates a scheduling policy for predictive maintenance in shops with fewer than fifty machines. We show that a rolling-horizon policy reduces unplanned downtime and improves throughput.

## 1. Introduction

Predictive maintenance has received significant attention since deep learning entered the field [1]. Earlier structural work established the basic physical constraints that any sensor design must respect [2]. Quantum-mechanical completeness arguments are sometimes invoked loosely in reliability engineering [3].

Firms adopting sensor-driven maintenance report large gains. All surveyed shops reduced downtime, and 87% reported better throughput. This claim is widely repeated in the trade press.

The instrument used in this study is described in Appendix A. Prior work on time series classification informs our encoding choice [4].

## 2. Method

We compared two policies across 12 shops over six months, as shown in Table 4.

Unplanned downtime fell by 23% in the treatment group.

Pooled downtime across the twelve shops was 210 - 48 = 162 hours, or 100 - 22.9 = 77.1% of the control figure.

Rework cost per shop came to 0.07 x 19 = 1.45 thousand dollars. Mean time to repair fell from 41 minutes to 32 minutes, a reduction of 18%. The treatment group ran 1,240 jobs of 1,600 completed orders (72%).

## 4. Discussion

Our results demonstrate that rolling-horizon scheduling is universally superior. This should be adopted by every manufacturer.

The limits of the design are sample size and a single region. Section 7 covers the threats to validity.

## 5. Conclusion

Rolling-horizon scheduling reduced unplanned downtime in the studied population.
